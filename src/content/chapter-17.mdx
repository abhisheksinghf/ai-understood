## 1. A vector is a representation, not a verdict

An **embedding** maps an item into a numerical vector. For text retrieval, an **encoder** turns a query or passage into a fixed number of coordinates. Similarity in that space is intended to reflect relationships useful for its training task.

An embedding is not the text's ID, a set of word counts, or a factuality score. Unlike the sparse TF-IDF coordinates in Chapter 16, a dense learned vector usually has many nonzero entries whose meaning is distributed across coordinates. “Coordinate 42 means adventure” is generally not a valid interpretation.

A good retrieval encoder may place “space rescue” near “astronauts save a stranded pilot.” That is learned behavior to test, not a guarantee of embeddings in general. Domain, language, wording, negation, and the model's training objective matter.

For this chapter's explorer, we **choose three-dimensional vectors by hand** for the eight familiar movies and three preset queries. They make geometry inspectable; they are not outputs of a neural encoder. A successful result on these authored coordinates cannot establish that a trained model outperforms keyword search.

## 2. Understand what the encoder learns

Many retrieval encoders learn from related and unrelated text pairs. A training example might pair “space rescue” with a useful summary as a **positive**, and with an unrelated café summary as a **negative**. A contrastive objective can reward higher similarity for the positive than for alternatives.

The learned parameters determine the representation. At inference time, the fixed encoder calculates a new vector; saving that vector to an index does not train the encoder again. Methods differ in their losses, training data, pooling, and use of negatives. [Sentence-BERT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084) is a primary example of learning reusable sentence representations for similarity comparisons.

A **bi-encoder** represents the query and document separately. This lets us precompute document vectors and compare them with many later queries. A query and its answer need not say the same thing: a retrieval model must capture usefulness for the intended task, not merely paraphrase similarity.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: tokens, pooling, and hard negatives</summary><p>A text encoder commonly tokenizes input, computes contextual token representations, then uses a pooling or selection rule to produce one passage vector. A token embedding, a whole-passage embedding, and an LLM's generated answer are different objects. Arbitrarily averaging hidden states from any language model does not ensure good retrieval.</p><p>A <strong>hard negative</strong> looks related but should not answer the query, such as a rescue mission on a city road for an outer-space request. It can teach finer distinctions. But a supposedly negative example may actually be relevant: false negatives introduce conflicting supervision. Training data quality and the intended retrieval task matter as much as the loss's name.</p><p>Input limits also matter. If a long synopsis is truncated, its rescue scene may never reach the encoder. Pooling compresses information too; a single vector does not retain every detail. Chapter 18 introduces passage boundaries and chunking in a full RAG pipeline.</p></details>

## 3. Keep queries and documents in a compatible space

Both sides must follow a compatible representation contract: model and version, input preparation, dimensionality, query/document roles, and any normalization. Some retrieval models require different prompts or paths for queries and passages. Follow that model's documented recipe rather than assuming the inputs are interchangeable. The [Sentence Transformers semantic-search guide](https://www.sbert.net/examples/sentence_transformer/applications/semantic-search/README.html) distinguishes symmetric similarity from short-query-to-passage retrieval.

**Equal dimensions are necessary, but not sufficient.** Two unrelated encoders can each return 384 numbers while organizing those coordinates differently. Comparing a query from one with stored vectors from the other has no established semantic meaning.

<EmbeddingDiagrams kind="pipeline" />

Store each vector with a stable source ID, source text or pointer, content revision, and embedding version. Re-encode changed text. When changing the encoder or its representation recipe, build a compatible replacement index and validate it before switching queries to it. The vector index organizes representations; it does not repair incompatible ones.

Our workbook records an explicit `space_id` and rejects mismatched IDs, wrong dimensions, nonfinite coordinates, and zero-length vectors. This catches declared contract failures, but a matching string cannot prove that someone actually used the claimed encoder.

## 4. Choose what close means

Chapter 6 introduced the arithmetic. Now it becomes a retrieval policy. For query vector $q$ and document vector $x$:

| Comparison | Formula | Preferred result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dot product | $q\cdot x=\sum_i q_i x_i$ | Larger |
| Cosine similarity | $(q\cdot x)/(\lVert q\rVert\lVert x\rVert)$ | Larger |
| Euclidean distance | $\sqrt{\sum_i(q_i-x_i)^2}$ | Smaller |

The **norm**, $\lVert x\rVert=\sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}$, is a vector's length. Cosine compares direction after dividing out lengths. Dot product is influenced by both direction and magnitude. Euclidean distance measures separation in the coordinate space.

<EmbeddingDiagrams kind="geometry" />

For our space query $q=[1,0,0]$, Quiet Orbit has $x=[0.9,0.1,0]$. Its dot product is 0.9, its norm is $\sqrt{0.82}$, and its cosine is $0.9/\sqrt{0.82}\approx\mathbf{0.9939}$. Its Euclidean distance is $\sqrt{0.02}\approx\mathbf{0.1414}$.

City Sprint's authored vector is $[3,2,0.2]$. Its raw dot product is **3**, so it beats Quiet Orbit under that rule despite being irrelevant to the outer-space intent. That exposes a representation/metric mismatch in this fixture. Dot product is not inherently wrong: use the comparison the real model was designed and evaluated for. See the [Faiss metric reference](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/MetricType-and-distances).

## 5. Normalize when the representation calls for it

**Unit normalization** divides a nonzero vector by its norm: $\hat{x}=x/\lVert x\rVert$. It preserves direction and makes length equal to one. It does not turn coordinates into probabilities or improve their semantic meaning automatically.

For unit query and document vectors:

$$
\hat{q}\cdot\hat{x}=\cos(q,x),\qquad
\lVert\hat{q}-\hat{x}\rVert^2=2-2\cos(q,x).
$$

Therefore descending dot product, descending cosine, and ascending Euclidean distance give the same ordering, apart from numerical ties. Without normalization they can disagree. Our raw Euclidean order is Quiet Orbit, Unlisted Voyage, Parallel World; raw cosine swaps the last two.

Cosine ranges from −1 to 1, but a value such as **0.99 is not a 99% chance of relevance**. Scores and useful thresholds depend on the encoder, data, and task. Even unrelated queries have nearest neighbors, so top-K alone does not prove that a good match exists.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: the identity, zero vectors, and numerical precision</summary><p>Expand squared distance: ‖a − b‖² = ‖a‖² + ‖b‖² − 2a·b. For unit vectors, the first two terms are both 1, giving 2 − 2a·b. This explains the ranking equivalence without needing a special library.</p><p>A zero vector has no direction, so cosine and unit normalization divide by zero. This workbook rejects norms below 10<sup>−12</sup> instead of assigning a made-up score. Floating-point results can differ in their last digits; we clamp cosine to [−1, 1] to handle rounding and break exact score ties by movie ID.</p><p>Some search libraries report <strong>squared</strong> Euclidean distance. It gives the same ordering as distance, but different values and thresholds. Our explorer displays the square root. Quantization stores approximate coordinates with fewer bits; it can change neighbors and needs measurement against an uncompressed reference.</p></details>

## 6. Distinguish a vector index from a vector database

An **exact search** compares the query with every eligible vector, then keeps the best K. With $N$ vectors and $d$ coordinates, the scoring work is proportional to $Nd$, before result selection. For eight movies, this is straightforward and provides a useful reference answer.

A **vector index** is a data structure or algorithm for finding neighbors. A **vector database** or a database with vector support adds storage, updates, metadata queries, durability, and other operational features. Storing numbers does not itself require a separate database product, and a database cannot make poor embeddings meaningful.

Choose infrastructure based on measured scale and requirements. For example, one million 768-dimensional vectors stored as float32 values require $1{,}000{,}000\times768\times4=$ **3,072,000,000 bytes**, about 3.07 GB in decimal units, for coordinates alone. IDs, indexes, metadata, source text, and working memory add more. Higher dimensions increase storage and scoring work without guaranteeing better retrieval.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: filters, updates, and stale representations</summary><p>A runtime requirement still belongs in metadata. Unlisted Voyage has unknown runtime, so it cannot satisfy “known runtime below 120 minutes,” however close its vector is. Keep permissions and other hard eligibility rules separate from similarity.</p><p>Applying filters after an approximate top-K search can leave too few results or miss eligible neighbors outside the initial candidate set. Filter-aware search, larger candidate pools, or iterative scans may help; behavior depends on the engine and index. The <a href="https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector">pgvector documentation</a> discusses exact search, approximate indexes, and filtering tradeoffs.</p><p>Keep vector and source revisions aligned. If a synopsis changes while its old vector remains, search and displayed evidence disagree. Deletion, deduplication, model upgrades, and query caching also need explicit handling. Our workbook rebuilds a tiny index for each run; it is not a persistent storage service.</p></details>

## 7. Trade search work for approximation deliberately

**Approximate nearest-neighbor search (ANN)** tries to find close vectors while examining fewer candidates. It may miss a vector that the exact search would return. This approximation error is separate from the embedding model's semantic errors.

One approach partitions vectors into cells around centroids and searches only a few promising cells. **IVF** indexes use this broad idea. Another approach, **HNSW**, searches through a graph of neighboring vectors at multiple levels. Their construction costs, memory use, and search settings differ. See the [Faiss index overview](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss/wiki/Faiss-indexes).

<EmbeddingDiagrams kind="index" />

Our small demonstration uses three **fixed, authored centroids**. Each movie is assigned to the centroid with highest cosine. The query selects the best one, two, or three cells, and we score eligible members inside them. It illustrates coarse candidate selection; it is not a trained IVF index or an implementation of HNSW.

For `space rescue`, exact cosine finds M004, M008, M007. The nearest cell A contains only M004 and M008. One probe therefore recovers two of the three exact neighbors; a second probe visits B and recovers M007. More probes increase work here. Real speed/quality choices require realistic queries, hardware, filters, and latency measurements.

## 8. Measure two different kinds of success

**Exact-neighbor recall@K** asks whether approximate search preserved the exact result under the same vectors, metric, and eligible set. We divide the number of recovered exact neighbors by the size of the exact top-K set. If fewer than K eligible movies exist, that set is smaller; if it is empty, the measure is undefined.

**Relevance recall@K**, from Chapter 16, asks whether the returned movies answer the search intent. Its denominator is the number of relevant eligible movies. A search can preserve every exact neighbor and still return irrelevant results because the representation or metric is unsuitable.

The authored space example makes exact cosine retrieve all three relevant movies, while BM25 retrieves two. But for `funny adventure`, cosine places Cafe Chaos above Moonlight Map: the nearest movie is funny without being the intended adventure. BM25 places Moonlight Map first. This fixture illustrates possible outcomes, not model performance evidence.

Keep a lexical baseline. On independent judged queries, inspect relevance metrics, missed facts, runtime, memory, and how often ANN loses exact neighbors. Query-specific failure analysis matters more than assuming that newer representation methods always win.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: hybrid retrieval and reranking</summary><p><strong>Hybrid retrieval</strong> combines candidates from lexical and vector search. Exact names, rare identifiers, and phrasing can favor lexical matching; paraphrases may favor a learned representation. Raw BM25 and cosine scores have different scales, so simply adding them needs a justified calibration strategy.</p><p><strong>Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF)</strong> instead sums 1/(c + rank) across input lists, using a chosen positive rank constant c. A document absent from a list contributes zero for that list. It combines rank evidence without treating the raw scores as comparable. Candidate cutoffs and c still affect the result; fusion is not a guarantee of improvement. See the <a href="https://www.elastic.co/docs/reference/elasticsearch/rest-apis/reciprocal-rank-fusion">RRF reference</a>.</p><p>A <strong>cross-encoder reranker</strong> reads a query and candidate together to score their relationship. Joint processing can capture interactions missed by independently encoded vectors, at extra computation per pair. It only reranks retrieved candidates; an omitted source needs better candidate retrieval. See the <a href="https://www.sbert.net/examples/sentence_transformer/applications/retrieve_rerank/README.html">retrieve-and-rerank guide</a>. Fusion and neural reranking are not implemented in this workbook.</p></details>

## 12. Bring the source text back into the system

You can now distinguish **encoder quality**, **vector geometry**, **index search quality**, and **task relevance**. A nearest-neighbor result is a set of source IDs and scores; a learning assistant still needs the actual passages, their provenance, and appropriate checks before making claims.

Embeddings can help find related text. They do not prove that a movie meets every constraint, that a source is current, or that a generated answer follows the source faithfully. Keep metadata, evidence, and evaluation beside the vectors.
