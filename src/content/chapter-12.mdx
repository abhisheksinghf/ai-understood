## 1. Decide what one row means

Chapter 11 selected movies using explicit rules. To learn from experience, we now need examples of previous viewing events. **One row represents one viewer watching one movie.** A movie watched by two viewers produces two different events.

Our question is: “Before this viewer watches, can we predict their later thumbs-up?” The **label**, or target, is the recorded outcome: `liked = 1` for a thumbs-up and `0` for a thumbs-down. The **features** are the inputs available when making that prediction.

<DataDiagrams kind="time" />

| Column | Meaning | Role in this workbook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `event_id`, `viewer_id`, `movie_id` | Which event, person, and film | Tracking and splitting; excluded from inputs |
| `runtime_minutes` | Movie length, sometimes missing | Numeric feature |
| `genre` | One primary genre | Categorical feature |
| `liked` | Feedback collected after watching | Label, kept separately |
| `review_after` | Text written after watching | Excluded: unavailable at prediction time |

Using that later review to predict the earlier decision would give the model information it will not have when deployed. This is **target leakage**. A column can be accurate and still be an invalid input.

Our two features are deliberately simple. Without viewer preferences or history, identical movie inputs cannot give different predictions for different viewers. This is a data-preparation exercise, not a complete personalized recommender. All records are synthetic.

## 2. Inspect before changing anything

A **schema** describes the expected fields and types. A **data contract** also specifies acceptable values and meanings: runtime is in whole minutes, IDs follow a defined format, and the label is blank, `0`, or `1`. Keep an unchanged raw copy so corrections can be traced.

The workbook begins with 13 raw rows. Inspect types, missing counts, ranges, category spellings, unique event IDs, and label counts. For example, `" Adventure "` and `"ADVENTURE"` should become the same genre; `"105"` must become a number before arithmetic.

| Raw value | Our explicit policy |
| --- | --- |
| `" Adventure "` | Trim whitespace; lowercase to `adventure` |
| `"105"` minutes | Parse to integer 105 |
| Blank runtime | Preserve as missing, not zero |
| `"-5"` minutes | Quarantine the invalid record |
| Blank `liked` | Exclude from labeled examples; never assume dislike |

**Quarantine** means set a record aside with its location and reason for investigation. The workbook accepts observed runtimes from 1 to 600 minutes as its declared scope. That limit is a chosen contract, not a universal definition of a movie.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: unusual values, units, and sampling bias</summary><p>An <strong>outlier</strong> is unusual relative to other values; it is not automatically wrong. A 200-minute movie can be legitimate, while a negative runtime violates the meaning of duration. Investigate provenance and units before deleting values. A runtime of 2 might mean two minutes or a mistaken hours-to-minutes conversion; the number alone cannot establish which. Data-driven outlier thresholds must be learned on training data, not chosen from the entire dataset.</p><p><strong>Sampling bias</strong> concerns who or what enters the data. Feedback from people who chose to watch a movie does not represent everybody who could have watched it. People who leave ratings may also differ from those who do not. Cleaning cannot fix this exposure and selection problem by itself. Keep the prediction population explicit, examine coverage, and record how labels were collected. Missing feedback is not a negative preference.</p></details>

## 3. Resolve duplicates without deleting real events

A repeated movie ID is normal: different viewers can watch the same film. A repeated **event ID** needs investigation. In this dataset, the second copy of E03 has the same normalized values, so retaining one copy avoids counting the event twice.

If valid records share an event ID but disagree, our policy quarantines all of them. Choosing whichever row happens to appear first would silently invent a resolution. A second viewing with its own event ID remains a legitimate separate observation.

After fixed checks, the audit reads: **13 raw rows → 10 labeled events**, with one duplicate copy removed, one negative-runtime row quarantined, and one unlabeled event set aside. The original missing runtimes remain missing at this stage.

These per-record contracts and identity checks use no estimated averages or fitted model. They can run before splitting. Decisions learned from the dataset—such as percentile cutoffs, feature selection, or a category vocabulary—belong inside the training process.

## 4. Split according to the question you will evaluate

The **training set** teaches the model and preprocessing. The **validation set** helps choose settings and compare approaches. The **test set** is reserved for evaluating the chosen approach. Repeatedly changing your system after seeing test results turns that set into tuning data.

Our evaluation question concerns **viewers absent from training**. All events from one viewer therefore stay in one split. Randomly shuffling individual rows could put the same viewer in both training and test, answering a different question.

<DataDiagrams kind="groups" />

This small plan puts U01–U03 in training, U04 in validation, and U05 in test. The 6/2/2 event counts are a teaching example, not a recommended ratio for every project. Two held-out events cannot support a reliable performance estimate.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: random, grouped, and time-based evaluation</summary><p>Choose the split to match deployment. A random row split can suit independent observations. A grouped split keeps related observations—such as a viewer's events—together. To predict future behavior, split by time and construct historical features using only information available before each event. This workbook has no timestamps and does not demonstrate time-based evaluation. To evaluate unseen movies, consider grouping by movie; keeping both new viewers and new movies separate requires a more careful design.</p><p><strong>Stratification</strong> tries to preserve class proportions; it does not by itself prevent group overlap or future information. In <strong>cross-validation</strong>, you rotate which training folds are held out, fitting preprocessing afresh inside each fold. Keep any final test set untouched. See scikit-learn's <a href="https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html">cross-validation and splitting guide</a>.</p></details>

## 5. Fill missing values without inventing facts

**Imputation** supplies a replacement value when a chosen model needs numeric input. Our training rows contain four observed runtimes: 90, 105, 115, and 140 minutes. Their median is the midpoint of the two middle values:

$$\text{training median}=\frac{105+115}{2}=110.$$

E04 and E06 have missing runtimes. We use 110 in their feature calculations and add a **missing indicator**: `runtime_missing = 1` when the original value was absent, otherwise 0. The raw runtime stays missing.

This replacement makes a vector usable; it does not establish that the movie lasts 110 minutes. Chapter 11's hard time-limit filter still cannot certify a film whose actual runtime is unknown. Keep modeling values separate from verified catalog facts.

Mean imputation would use `(90 + 105 + 115 + 140) / 4 = 112.5`. The workbench lets you compare both methods. Median imputation is less affected by extreme observed values, but neither method recovers the missing truth.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: missingness is a modeling decision</summary><p>Dropping every incomplete row can shrink the dataset and change who it represents. Filling a constant, mean, median, or a learned estimate makes different assumptions. A missing indicator preserves information about absence but does not automatically remove bias. More elaborate approaches can use other features to estimate missing values, and some estimators support missing values directly. See scikit-learn's <a href="https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/impute.html">imputation guide</a>.</p><p>If every training runtime is missing, there is no observed training median to calculate. The workbook stops and asks for an explicit alternative policy; it never borrows the test-set median. Missing labels are a separate problem: inventing a label creates invented supervision. Our pipeline sets those events aside rather than using feature imputation on the target.</p></details>

## 6. Encode categories and scale numbers

Most basic estimators expect a numeric **feature matrix**, $X$, with one row per example and one column per feature. The corresponding labels form $y$. Keep their ordering aligned: row 4 of $X$ must still match label 4 of $y$.

For genre, **one-hot encoding** creates separate indicator columns. An adventure event has 1 in `genre=adventure` and 0 in the other genre columns. Assigning arbitrary numbers such as comedy = 1 and drama = 2 would introduce an order or distance that the genre names do not imply.

Our training vocabulary has five genres. We reserve a sixth, `genre=__unknown__`, for unseen genres. Documentary appears only in held-out data, so it activates that bucket. The column order and count stay fixed.

For runtime, **standardization** subtracts the training mean and divides by the training standard deviation, both computed after filling missing training values:

$$z=\frac{x-\mu_{\text{train}}}{\sigma_{\text{train}}}.$$

With median filling, the six training runtimes are `105, 140, 115, 110, 90, 110`. Their mean is about 111.6667 and their population standard deviation is about 14.9071. E04's filled runtime becomes `(110 − 111.6667) / 14.9071 ≈ −0.1118`. A negative standardized value means below the training mean, not a negative duration.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: which transformations help which models?</summary><p>Scaling matters when an algorithm responds to feature magnitude, distance, or penalties. It is often useful for distance-based methods and regularized linear models; ordinary decision-tree splits generally do not require it. Standardization changes location and scale, not the distribution into a bell curve. This workbook divides squared deviations by the number of training rows, using population standard deviation; Chapter 7's sample estimate has a different purpose. A constant column uses a scale of 1 to avoid division by zero.</p><p>Min–max scaling uses a training range; robust scaling uses less outlier-sensitive statistics. Ordered categories can sometimes use an explicit ordinal mapping, while unordered categories need a suitable encoding. Our reserved unknown column is a deliberate convention, not the default behavior of every encoder. Fit and reuse the full transformation policy consistently. See scikit-learn's <a href="https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/preprocessing.html">preprocessing guide</a>.</p></details>

## 7. Separate fitting from transformation

**Fit** means learn preprocessing values from training rows: the replacement value, mean, standard deviation, and genre vocabulary. **Transform** means apply those already learned values to a row. A preprocessing step can be fitted even before a predictive model exists.

<DataDiagrams kind="fit" />

Training, validation, test, and future inputs must use the same fitted transformation. Computing a separate test-set mean would both peek at held-out data and put that set into a different coordinate system.

Our pipeline order is **fill runtime → standardize runtime → add the missing flag and genre indicators**. The resulting training matrix has shape **6 × 8**: two runtime-related features and six genre columns. Validation and test each have shape **2 × 8**. IDs, labels, and later reviews stay outside $X$.

```python
state = fit_preprocessor(splits["train"], "median")
train = transform_rows(splits["train"], state)
validation = transform_rows(splits["validation"], state)
test = transform_rows(splits["test"], state)
```

Fitting on all rows first is **preprocessing leakage**, even if the labels were never used. This separation is also the central advice in scikit-learn's [common pitfalls guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html).

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: feature engineering and reusable pipelines</summary><p><strong>Feature engineering</strong> constructs useful inputs, such as a missing flag, genre indicators, or a viewer's average rating of previously watched comedies. For a historical average, “previously” must mean before that example's prediction time; using later ratings leaks future information. Choosing which features to retain must also happen within training and validation, not by optimizing on final test results.</p><p>A pandas <strong>DataFrame</strong> is a labeled table useful for inspection and manipulation. Scikit-learn's <strong>ColumnTransformer</strong> can apply different transformations to numeric and categorical columns; a <strong>Pipeline</strong> connects preprocessing with an estimator. These tools help keep fitted steps together, including inside cross-validation. The standard-library workbook exposes the same fit/transform boundary directly so you can inspect every calculation without installing those packages.</p></details>

## 12. Keep the preparation story straight

You should now be able to explain this chain: **define the event and prediction time → inspect and check records → choose a deployment-relevant split → fit preprocessing on training only → reuse it everywhere → verify the boundaries**.

Before fitting a model, ask whether the label means what you intend, the inputs would exist at prediction time, and evaluation examples represent the question you care about. A clean-looking table is necessary work; it is not proof of representative data or a useful model.
