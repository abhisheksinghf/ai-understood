## 1. Learning starts with a task and examples

Suppose an operations assistant must estimate a job's runtime **before the job starts**. The input is job size; the output is a number of seconds. Predicting a numerical value is a **regression** task. Predicting a category, as in Chapter 2, is **classification**.

A **dataset** is a collection of examples. Each row below describes one fictional completed job. The **feature** is information supplied to the model; the **label** or **target** is the answer it should learn to predict.

| Training example | Feature x: size, in thousands of records | Target y: actual runtime, in seconds |
|---|---|---|
| A | 1 | 3 |
| B | 2 | 5 |
| C | 3 | 7 |

These three rows are deliberately clean **synthetic data**, created to make the arithmetic visible. They are not enough to establish a useful production predictor. Real runtime also depends on hardware, competing work, and the kind of processing.

The prediction moment matters. Job size may be available before execution; the eventual finish time is not. A model trained with information unavailable at that moment cannot be evaluated fairly for the intended use.

## 2. A prediction becomes a measurable error

Choose a simple model:

$$
\hat{y}=wx+1
$$

Read this as: **predicted runtime = weight × job size + 1 second**. The hat on y means “predicted.” The **weight w** is a parameter we will learn. Here the constant +1 is fixed; in a more general straight-line model, the intercept can also be learned.

Start at w = 1. The three predictions are 2, 3, and 4 seconds. Compare each with its actual value:

| Example | Predicted | Actual | Error: predicted − actual | Squared error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2 | 3 | −1 | 1 |
| B | 3 | 5 | −2 | 4 |
| C | 4 | 7 | −3 | 9 |

A **loss function** gives a numerical penalty for predictions. We will use **mean squared error (MSE)**: square each error, add the results, and divide by the number of examples.

$$
\text{MSE}=\frac{1+4+9}{3}=\frac{14}{3}\approx4.67\ \text{seconds}^2
$$

Squaring prevents positive and negative errors from cancelling and gives larger errors more influence. The units are squared seconds, not seconds. At w = 2, the predictions become 3, 5, and 7, so this training MSE is zero.

That is a perfect fit to **these three rows**. It does not establish perfect performance on future jobs. [Google's loss lesson](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/linear-regression/loss) explains common regression losses.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: loss, objective, and evaluation metric</summary>

A training **objective** is what the learning procedure tries to optimize. It can be the average loss alone or include other terms, such as a penalty discouraging excessive model complexity.

An **evaluation metric** is a measure used to judge the result. It can match the loss, but need not. Here, mean absolute error (MAE) at w = 1 is (1 + 2 + 3) / 3 = 2 seconds. MAE is easier to interpret in the original units; MSE gives large errors more weight.

The chosen metric must also reflect the task. A small average error might hide a few severely underestimated runtimes. Examine errors on large jobs and any cases that matter operationally. Classification and language modeling often use different losses; not every model is trained with MSE.

</details>

## 3. The training loop changes parameters

The model does not need a person to manually try every possible weight. An **optimizer** is a procedure for adjusting parameters to improve the objective.

<LearningDiagrams kind="loop" />

One common optimizer is **gradient descent**. The **gradient** describes how the objective changes as parameters change. Gradient descent moves parameters in the opposite direction, with the step size controlled by a **learning rate**.

For our example, increasing w from 1 toward 2 reduces the training loss. An appropriate update moves in that direction. A step that is too large can cross the low-loss region and make the result worse.

The distinction is useful: **w is a learned parameter; the learning rate is a hyperparameter**, a setting of the learning procedure. [Google's gradient descent lesson](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/linear-regression/gradient-descent) develops this update mechanism.

A **batch** is the set of examples used for one update. An **epoch** is one pass through the training set. Our tiny experiment uses all three examples per update, so one step is also one epoch here. With many batches, one epoch usually contains many steps.

Training repeatedly predicts, compares, and adjusts. **Inference** uses the fitted parameter to predict a new runtime. Reading that output, or supplying a new input, does not by itself update the weight.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Optional mathematics: calculate the first update</summary>

For this particular dataset, the loss as a function of w is:

$$
L(w)=\frac{14}{3}(w-2)^2
\qquad
\frac{dL}{dw}=\frac{28}{3}(w-2)
$$

The derivative is the slope of the loss with respect to w. At w = 1 it is −28/3, approximately −9.3333. A gradient descent update with learning rate 0.10 gives:

$$
w_{\text{new}}=w-0.10\frac{dL}{dw}
=1-0.10(-9.3333)\approx1.9333
$$

The new training MSE is approximately 0.0207 squared seconds. A fresh start with rate 0.25 instead gives w ≈ 3.3333 and MSE ≈ 8.2963: worse than the initial 4.6667.

The interface displays rounded values but calculates with full numerical precision. These learning rates make sense for this tiny, scaled dataset; they are not universal recommendations.

Neural networks extend the idea to many parameters. **Backpropagation** efficiently computes the gradients through the network; the optimizer uses those gradients to update parameters. We will derive this after the mathematical foundations. Some ML methods, such as ordinary decision-tree training, use other procedures.

</details>

## 5. Give training, validation, and testing different jobs

**Generalization** means useful performance on relevant cases beyond those used to fit the model. To estimate it, keep some examples separate.

<LearningDiagrams kind="split" />

- **Training set:** fit parameters. Any data-dependent preparation, such as learning feature averages for normalization, must also be fitted on the training portion.
- **Validation set:** compare choices such as model size, learning rate, and when to stop. It influences development choices, even if it is not used in gradient updates.
- **Test set:** assess the selected system after those choices are fixed. Repeatedly tuning to its results turns it into development data.

There is no universally correct split percentage. The split should imitate the use case. For future job prediction, train on earlier jobs and evaluate on later periods. If near-duplicate jobs from one run appear in both sets, apparent performance may be misleading.

**Data leakage** happens when information that should be unavailable enters training or model selection. Using a job's actual finish time as an input to a pre-start prediction is leakage. Fitting preprocessing on all examples before splitting can also leak information. [scikit-learn's common pitfalls guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/common_pitfalls.html#data-leakage) explains prevention.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: validation is useful, but not unlimited evidence</summary>

Trying many alternatives on one validation set can gradually overfit your choices to that set. A separate final test helps reveal this. Report the size and scope of the evaluation; two validation rows, as in our experiment, are only an illustration.

**Cross-validation** rotates which development examples are used for fitting and validation across several splits. It can reduce dependence on one split, but it does not excuse leakage or remove the need for an appropriate final evaluation.

Respect time and grouping during splitting. Several records from the same viewer, movie, or processing batch may be dependent. The split must match whether deployment will encounter new groups, future records from existing groups, or both.

</details>

## 6. Underfitting and overfitting

**Underfitting** means the fitted model misses useful structure. A constant runtime estimate may miss the relationship between job size and runtime. Both training and validation errors can remain high. Causes can include an unsuitable model, missing features, or insufficient optimization.

**Overfitting** means fitting peculiarities of the training examples that do not help on relevant new cases. A flexible model might memorize individual job IDs or noisy timing fluctuations. Its training error can be excellent while validation error is worse.

<LearningDiagrams kind="fit" />

The graph shows one possible warning pattern: training loss keeps falling while validation loss starts rising. It is an illustration, not a law that every real learning curve must follow. A validation gap can also reflect different data distributions or a flawed split.

Possible responses include a simpler model, better or more representative data, **regularization** that discourages some kinds of complexity, and **early stopping** at a checkpoint selected using validation performance. More training is not automatically the cure. [Google's overfitting lesson](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/overfitting/overfitting) explains the distinction.

Do not diagnose from one score alone. Compare training and validation results, inspect individual mistakes, and check that the evaluation setup represents the intended task.

## 7. Why a model can still be wrong

Poor performance is not one problem with one fix. Use the error to decide what to investigate.

| What you observe | Possible explanation | A useful check |
|---|---|---|
| Similar jobs have different runtimes | Measurement noise or missing information, such as server load | Check measurement definitions and available features |
| Good average results, poor results on large jobs | Those jobs may be rare in training | Compare error and sample count by job-size group |
| Excellent offline results, poor live results | Leakage or mismatched preparation | Reproduce exactly what was available at prediction time |
| Results worsen after a hardware change | **Distribution shift:** deployment data or relationships differ | Compare recent inputs and errors with the development period |
| The model uses a job name as a shortcut | A correlation may not remain useful outside the sample | Test across job families and relevant future periods |

A relationship in data is not automatically causal. Larger jobs may often run overnight on faster machines. Changing the job size alone may not reproduce the association learned from that mixture.

Also distinguish estimation from certainty. Our line gives a point estimate, not a guarantee or an uncertainty interval. A plausible-looking number can still be wrong.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: bias and uncertainty are not single ideas</summary>

In model fitting, **bias** can mean systematic error from restrictive assumptions; it can also mean the intercept parameter in a linear model. In discussion of people and decisions, bias often concerns unfair or unrepresentative behavior. State which meaning you intend.

Some uncertainty comes from limited knowledge or insufficient data. Some comes from variability not captured by the inputs. Better data or features may reduce error, but repeated observations need not produce identical outcomes.

For classification, a model's numeric score is not automatically a calibrated probability. For regression, an uncertainty interval needs its own method and coverage checks. Later evaluation chapters develop these ideas.

</details>

## 8. The learning signal can take different forms

The runtime example uses **supervised learning**: inputs are paired with target answers. The broader learning landscape includes other signals:

| Approach | Where the signal comes from | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Supervised learning | Target values or categories supplied with examples | Job size paired with measured runtime |
| Self-supervised learning | Targets constructed from the data itself | Use earlier text to predict a later token |
| Unsupervised learning | Structure in data without supplied task labels | Group movie reviews by similar patterns |
| Reinforcement learning | Rewards from actions and outcomes during interaction | Learn a scheduling policy in a simulator |

These categories are useful descriptions, not perfectly exclusive boxes. Self-supervised learning is often discussed within unsupervised learning, and a system can combine several approaches.

Reward also differs from a correct answer for every step. In reinforcement learning, a useful outcome can depend on a sequence of actions. The training setup must connect outcomes to those actions.

The common thread is an objective or learning signal shaping behavior. Success against that signal still needs to be checked against the actual task.

## 9. How this connects to LLMs and wrong answers

In a common language-model training setup, the target is the next token already present in a text example. The model estimates token probabilities; a loss penalizes assigning too little probability to the target. This is a self-supervised training signal. [Hugging Face's training overview](https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/chapter1/4) describes this approach.

Later training can use instruction examples or feedback about preferred responses. Those stages change the training data or objectives. They do not make every generated claim true.

A language model's learned patterns may not contain a current fact, may reflect an unreliable source, or may not be enough to resolve an ambiguous request. During generation it can produce a fluent continuation that is incorrect or unsupported: a **hallucination**.

For our movie assistant, missing streaming availability should remain unknown. A plausible service name is not established by fluent wording. Supplying a catalog entry through RAG changes the model's context; fine-tuning changes its parameters. Both still need evaluation.

**The practical habit:** name the symptom before changing the system. Missing documents suggest investigating retrieval. Incorrect arithmetic suggests using exact code. A misleading offline score suggests checking evaluation and leakage. Do not assume every failure requires a bigger model.
