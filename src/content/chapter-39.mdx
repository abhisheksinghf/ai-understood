## 1. Start with the decision the application must support

A useful design begins with an **input, output, decision, and cost of error**. “Use AI for our cinema” does not yet specify a learning problem.

| Feature | Input and output | Decision supported | A meaningful mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Poster finder | Image → poster locations | Which region should be cropped for identification? | Missing a small poster or returning two boxes for one poster. |
| Movie recommender | Viewer history + eligible catalog → ranked list | What should appear in the first few positions? | Ranking a poor fit first, or offering an unavailable movie. |
| Demand forecast | Earlier daily ticket counts → future counts | How much capacity should tomorrow’s schedule provide? | Underestimating a busy day or staffing for demand that never arrives. |

The **unit of evaluation** changes: objects in images, ranked items per viewer, or forecasts at specific times and horizons. One generic “accuracy” score cannot describe all three. Keep a simple baseline and an explicit evaluation contract for each, building on Chapter 34.

Our lab uses authored boxes, movie scores, and 21 daily counts. It calculates real matching, ranking, and forecasting metrics. It does not train or run a vision network or a collaborative-filtering model.

## 2. Turn pixels into the output the task needs

An image is a grid of numerical values, usually with color channels. A vision model learns features that connect those values to a target. Chapter 22 introduced CNNs and transfer learning; the application still needs the right kind of target.

| Vision task | Question | Cinema example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classification | What label describes this image or crop? | Classify a cropped poster into a catalog category. |
| Object detection | What objects occur, and where? | Return a box and class for each poster on a wall. |
| Semantic segmentation | What class does each pixel belong to? | Label every poster pixel, without separating individual posters. |
| Instance segmentation | Which pixels belong to each individual object? | Separate two overlapping posters with two masks. |
| OCR / image retrieval | What text is visible / which catalog image is similar? | Read a title, or find a candidate catalog match from a crop. |

<AppliedSystemsDiagrams kind="vision" />

A **bounding box** encloses an object; a **mask** labels its pixels. Detection can locate a poster without knowing its movie title. A later OCR or image-embedding retrieval step can propose catalog identities, followed by validation. Similar artwork or unreadable text can still produce ambiguity. See the [TorchVision detection and segmentation tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/intermediate/torchvision_tutorial.html) for a concrete image/box/mask training workflow.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: data, transfer learning, and image transformations</summary>

Start with labeled examples that represent actual use: camera angles, small objects, glare, blur, and lighting. Use the same box and class definitions across annotators. A pretrained backbone can supply visual features; a task head learns the required outputs. Freezing a backbone trains only the head, while fine-tuning updates selected pretrained weights too.

Resize, crop, or flip an image together with its boxes and masks. A crop can remove an object, and a flip changes coordinates. Keep related frames from one recording together when splitting data; almost identical frames on both sides can inflate evaluation. Also inspect separate cameras or locations when deployment must generalize to them. These are application-specific versions of the leakage and transfer-learning lessons from Chapters 12 and 22.

</details>

## 3. Measure whether the detected object is in the right place

The lab stores boxes as **[left, top, right, bottom]** coordinates. **Intersection over union (IoU)** measures overlap:

$$
\operatorname{IoU}(A,B)=\frac{\operatorname{area}(A\cap B)}{\operatorname{area}(A\cup B)}.
$$

Identical boxes have IoU 1; nonoverlapping boxes have 0. For two 10-by-10 boxes shifted horizontally by 2, the intersection is 80 and the union is 120, so IoU is $80/120\approx0.667$. This measures location agreement, not how confident the detector feels. See the [TorchVision IoU definition](https://docs.pytorch.org/vision/stable/generated/torchvision.ops.box_iou.html).

Our simplified **single-class evaluation** keeps predictions whose score meets a threshold, processes them from highest score downward, and matches each to the best still-unmatched truth box in the same frame with IoU at least 0.5. Each truth object can be matched once. A matched prediction is a true positive (TP); an unmatched prediction is a false positive (FP); an unmatched truth object is a false negative (FN).

At score threshold **0.6**, five predictions survive: **3 TP, 2 FP, and 1 FN**. Precision is $3/5=60\%$; recall is $3/4=75\%$. P2 overlaps an already matched object, so it is a duplicate FP. P7 has a high score but matches no object.

The **score threshold** decides which predictions to keep; the **IoU threshold** decides whether their locations count as correct. Raising the score threshold to 0.9 leaves one TP and one FP here: precision falls to 50% and recall to 25%. A higher threshold does not guarantee higher precision on a finite dataset, and a score is not automatically a calibrated probability.

Real detector evaluations also use average precision (AP), summarizing precision/recall across score cutoffs; **Mean average precision (mAP)** averages AP across classes and, in some protocols, IoU thresholds. Always state the protocol. [Non-maximum suppression](https://docs.pytorch.org/vision/stable/generated/torchvision.ops.nms.html) can remove overlapping duplicate predictions before evaluation; it is different from matching predictions to ground truth. This lab intentionally retains a duplicate so the rule is visible.

## 4. Build recommendations from candidates to a ranked list

**Content-based recommendations** compare item properties with a viewer’s interests. A viewer who liked gentle adventures may like another movie with similar attributes. **Collaborative filtering** learns from interaction patterns across users and items; it can discover a movie that looks different in metadata but appeals to similar viewers. **Hybrid systems** combine signals. See Google’s introductions to [candidate generation](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/recommendation/overview/candidate-generation) and [collaborative filtering](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/recommendation/collaborative/basics).

<AppliedSystemsDiagrams kind="recommend" />

Candidate generation retrieves a manageable pool. A ranker scores the candidates using richer context. Re-ranking applies list-level goals such as variety and freshness, while hard eligibility rules enforce availability and other requirements. A ranker cannot recover a relevant item that candidate generation omitted. This follows the [candidate, scoring, and re-ranking architecture](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/recommendation/overview/types).

In our lab, **all five eligible movies are candidates**. Already watched and unavailable items are excluded before scoring. A hand-authored interest vector is compared with movie feature vectors by cosine similarity. The other input is an authored collaborative-style score, not a score fitted from user logs. Both are on a 0–1 teaching scale:

$$
s(u,i)=\lambda\,s_{\text{content}}(u,i)+(1-\lambda)\,s_{\text{collaborative}}(u,i).
$$

For *Starlit Journey*, content is 0.6 and the collaborative-style score is 0.9. At $\lambda=0.5$, the combined score is **0.75**. Higher ranks win; exact ties use movie ID. In production, signals need comparable scales or a learned ranker; averaging arbitrary raw scores is not a general recipe.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: latent factors, implicit feedback, and cold start</summary>

Matrix factorization represents each user and item with learned latent vectors; their dot product estimates affinity. The factors need not correspond to named genres. Explicit feedback might be a rating; implicit feedback might be a watch or click. Missing feedback does not prove dislike: the viewer may never have seen the item. See Google’s [matrix-factorization explanation](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/recommendation/collaborative/matrix).

**Cold start** means too little history for a user or item. New-user onboarding, contextual or broadly useful defaults, and content features can provide initial candidates. A new movie can have a useful description before it has interactions. The lab’s feature names are deliberately interpretable; learned embeddings usually need a different interpretation. No LLM is required to compute these ranking scores. An LLM may help explain a verified recommendation, but its explanation does not establish that the ranking is good.

</details>

## 5. Evaluate the list the viewer actually receives

Choose a relevance definition and a cutoff **K**. Here, two eligible movies are marked relevant in an authored later evaluation snapshot. Those labels enter the metrics only, never the ranking score.

$$
\mathrm{Precision@K}=\frac{\text{relevant items in top K}}{K},\qquad
\mathrm{Recall@K}=\frac{\text{relevant items in top K}}{\text{all relevant eligible items}}.
$$

At the default blend, the top two are *Starlit Journey* (relevant) and *Quiet Orbit* (not relevant). Precision@2 and Recall@2 are both **0.5**. The ideal top two would contain both relevant movies.

**Normalized discounted cumulative gain (NDCG@K)** rewards putting relevant items earlier. For our binary labels, each relevant item at position $r$ contributes $1/\log_2(r+1)$. Divide this discounted gain by the gain of the ideal ordering. A relevant item first and an irrelevant item second gives $1/(1+1/\log_2 3)\approx\mathbf{0.613147}$. Reversing them gives **0.386853**, even though precision and recall are unchanged. See the [NDCG metric documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.ndcg_score.html).

Increasing K to 3 with the default blend includes *Harbor Lights*: precision becomes 2/3 and recall 1. Different list sizes serve different user experiences. Compare systems at an agreed K and candidate universe; use multiple viewers, and state how users with no relevant items are handled. Our evaluator returns undefined for recall/NDCG if no eligible relevant items exist.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: exposure bias and feedback loops</summary>

An unclicked movie may be irrelevant, unseen, or simply ranked too low to notice. Training directly on clicks can reinforce earlier exposure and popularity. A recommender changes what users see, which changes the next training dataset. Google’s [scoring guide](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/recommendation/dnn/scoring) discusses objectives and position bias.

Use time-aware evaluation with historical features and later outcomes, inspect new users/items and other important groups, and monitor diversity, catalog coverage, unwanted repetition, and satisfaction. Carefully designed online experiments measure effects that offline ranking scores alone cannot establish. Our one-viewer example illustrates arithmetic, not recommendation quality for a population. Chapter 38’s warning about optimizing a proxy applies here too.

</details>

## 6. Represent demand as a time series

A **time series** is a sequence of observations indexed by time. Specify what is counted, the sampling interval, and when each value becomes available. “Daily ticket demand” could mean bookings made that day, tickets for that day’s shows, or actual attendance; these are different targets.

**Trend** is longer-term movement. **Seasonality** repeats at a known period, such as a weekly pattern. The **remainder** is what the chosen decomposition leaves unexplained; it is not proof of meaningless noise. See [time-series components](https://otexts.com/fpp3/components.html). A 21-day example can demonstrate a weekly pattern, but cannot establish yearly seasonality.

For a forecast made after day $t$, a lag-one feature is $y_t$, and a trailing-three-day mean is $(y_t+y_{t-1}+y_{t-2})/3$. Forecasting tomorrow must not use tomorrow’s count. A centered moving average that includes future observations would leak information.

Calendar features such as day of week may be known in advance. Actual future weather or final bookings are not automatically known at forecast time; use only the version available then. Distinguish a missing report from zero tickets and from a closed cinema. Consistent timestamps, time zones, and reporting cutoffs are part of the modeling problem.

## 7. Forecast with baselines and honest backtests

Start with forecasts you can explain. A **last-value baseline** predicts tomorrow equals today. A **seasonal-naive baseline** predicts tomorrow equals the same weekday last week. Our **trailing mean of three** averages the last three observed days. Simple baselines are essential comparisons for more complex methods; see [simple forecasting methods](https://otexts.com/fpp3/simple-methods.html).

<AppliedSystemsDiagrams kind="time" />

The **forecast origin** is when the prediction is made; the **horizon** is how far ahead it predicts. Our lab evaluates seven **one-day-ahead** forecasts, for days 15–21. To predict day 15, it uses observations through day 14. After day 15 is observed, it may use that value to predict day 16. This is a [rolling-origin backtest](https://otexts.com/fpp3/tscv.html), not seven forecasts all issued on day 14.

For day 15, last value predicts **105**; trailing mean predicts **108.333333**; seasonal naive uses day 8 and predicts **45**. The actual count is **50**. The weekly baseline handles this deliberately weekly dataset well; that is not evidence that it wins on every cinema.

Freeze each method before scoring a fold. Any fitted scaler, imputer, feature selector, or model must use only information available at that origin. Tuning repeatedly on these seven displayed outcomes turns them into development data; keep a later untouched period for a final assessment. Randomly shuffling rows is generally unsuitable for estimating future forecasting performance when it allows later information into earlier predictions.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: statistical, feature-based, and neural forecasters</summary>

Exponential smoothing gives more weight to recent observations and can model level, trend, and seasonality. ARIMA (autoregressive integrated moving average) models dependence on earlier values and past errors, with differencing used to address certain nonstationary patterns; seasonal extensions handle periodic structure. Roughly, stationarity means statistical properties remain stable over time. ETS (error, trend, seasonal) models and ARIMA offer different representations; see the [ETS/ARIMA comparison](https://otexts.com/fpp3/arima-ets.html).

A regression or tree model can use lagged counts, calendar variables, and other available predictors. Neural sequence models can share patterns across many related series. Data volume, noise, changing conditions, and the forecast horizon determine whether added complexity helps. Compare all candidates under the same information cutoff. For a seven-day forecast issued today, later actual counts remain unknown: use an appropriate multi-step method and evaluate each horizon. Do not silently substitute observed future counts between those steps.

</details>

## 8. Connect forecast errors to decisions and uncertainty

For actual $y_t$ and forecast $\hat y_t$, define error $e_t=y_t-\hat y_t$. Positive error means underprediction here. **MAE** averages absolute errors; **RMSE** takes the square root of mean squared errors and gives large misses more influence. Both have the target’s units. See [forecast accuracy measures](https://otexts.com/fpp3/accuracy.html).

| Lab method | MAE over days 15–21 | RMSE | What the result means here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Last value | 22.143 tickets | 27.710 | Yesterday can be a poor guide across weekday changes. |
| Trailing mean of three | 31.905 tickets | 37.054 | Smoothing the recent weekend can overpredict the next quiet day. |
| Seasonal naive, lag seven | 5.000 tickets | 5.000 | Every forecast is five below the actual in this authored series. |

MAE 5 does not mean every possible future error is at most 5. MAPE divides by actual values and becomes problematic at or near zero; it is not a universal replacement. Evaluate the required horizons, peaks, quiet periods, and different cinemas, and keep measurement units visible.

A forecast estimates demand; a scheduling policy decides capacity using costs and constraints. Likewise, a detected poster still needs identity validation, and a recommendation must satisfy eligibility rules. For each application, preserve the input version, prediction, decision, later outcome, and time needed to detect performance changes.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Go deeper: prediction intervals and changes in demand</summary>

A **prediction interval** describes uncertainty about a future observation under a model and its assumptions. It is different from a confidence interval for an estimated parameter. A nominal 90% interval should be assessed for roughly 90% coverage on comparable future cases, alongside its width; an extremely wide interval may cover well but be unhelpful. See [prediction intervals](https://otexts.com/fpp3/prediction-intervals.html).

New releases, a changed timetable, holidays, or a closure can alter demand. Track recent errors and missing inputs; investigate shifts before simply retraining. Underprediction and overprediction may have different operational costs, so a chosen quantile can be more useful than a single central estimate. Our seven toy errors cannot support a reliable general uncertainty claim; the lab intentionally reports point forecasts without invented intervals.

</details>
