## 1. Start with the outcome you want to predict

**Supervised learning** uses examples that pair inputs with known outcomes. For a historical movie-viewing event, the input describes what was known before watching; the outcome records later feedback. Training adjusts model parameters to reduce a chosen loss on those examples. Inference applies the fitted model to a new input whose outcome is not yet known.

**Regression** predicts a numerical quantity, such as a rating of 3.93. **Classification** predicts membership in a category, such as thumbs-up or thumbs-down. A classifier may first estimate class probabilities and then apply a decision rule.

<SupervisedDiagrams kind="tasks" />

The target's meaning determines the task. A movie ID such as 104 is an identifier, not a quantity to predict with regression merely because it is written as a number. Likewise, a probability between 0 and 1 can be an intermediate output of a classification model.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: binary, multiclass, multilabel, and ordinal targets</summary><p><strong>Binary classification</strong> has two classes, such as liked/not liked. <strong>Multiclass classification</strong> chooses one class from several, such as one primary genre. <strong>Multilabel classification</strong> allows several labels at once: a film could be both adventure and comedy. These tasks require different target representations and output rules.</p><p>A one-to-five star rating is <strong>ordinal</strong>: the order is meaningful, but equal gaps between adjacent ratings are an assumption. Our regression exercise treats rating differences as numerical distances. That is a modeling choice, not a fact about human taste; an ordinal model or a class-based formulation could suit another product. Neither approach makes a predicted rating an observed fact.</p></details>

## 2. Give each model a clear input and label

Chapter 12 introduced the preparation boundary. We keep it: separate viewers, fit only on training examples, and exclude later feedback from features. This chapter uses a **new synthetic snapshot** with a history feature and two labels; it does not add invented ratings to Chapter 12's original records.

The input $s$ is the fraction of a viewer's **earlier rated movies in this genre** that received a thumbs-up. For example, three likes among four earlier rated adventure films gives $s=0.75$. This is observed history, not the model's probability for the next film.

| Training event | Past fraction $s$ | Later rating | Later thumbs-up |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S01 | 0.0 | 1 | 0 |
| S02 | 0.2 | 2 | 0 |
| S03 | 0.4 | 3 | 1 |
| S04 | 0.6 | 3 | 0 |
| S05 | 0.8 | 4 | 1 |
| S06 | 1.0 | 5 | 1 |

Each event belongs to a different viewer. Three additional viewers supply validation events and three supply test events. Rating and thumbs-up are separately recorded feedback; the thumbs-up label is not calculated by thresholding the rating. The two three-star events deliberately have different thumbs-up labels.

We transform $s$ into $x=2s-1$, putting the input between −1 and 1. This fixed formula uses no estimated dataset statistics. At $s=0.75$, $x=0.5$. Both models receive only this single feature, not the labels or viewer IDs.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: historical features need a time boundary</summary><p>In a real system, compute the fraction from feedback available before the target viewing event. Including that event's own outcome—or later feedback—leaks the answer. A viewer can be absent from model training yet bring legitimate earlier history at prediction time. That is the deployment situation this grouped example represents; it is not a no-history cold-start experiment.</p><p>The workbook supplies authored fractions, not raw timestamped histories, so it cannot verify their provenance. It also omits history counts: 3/4 and 300/400 both produce 0.75 but have different uncertainty. With zero earlier rated movies, the fraction is undefined; do not silently set it to zero. A production design needs a missing-history policy, suitable features, and evaluation for that population.</p></details>

## 3. Fit a line for a numerical prediction

Our **linear regression** model is:

$$
\hat y=wx+b.
$$

The hat means “predicted.” The weight $w$ controls how the prediction changes with $x$; the intercept $b$ is the prediction at $x=0$. These are learned **parameters**. With several features, the model becomes $\hat y=w_1x_1+\cdots+w_dx_d+b$, connecting directly to Chapter 6's dot product.

Without regularization, fitting our six rows gives $w=13/7\approx1.8571$ and $b=3$. For a viewer with $s=0.75$, the prediction is:

$$
\hat y=\frac{13}{7}(0.5)+3\approx3.9286.
$$

The line summarizes a tendency rather than passing through every point. It predicts the same value for identical inputs, even when different viewers later react differently. One history feature cannot describe every reason a person likes a film.

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: where the fitted numbers come from</summary><p>For one nonconstant feature, least squares has a direct solution: divide the sum of centered input–target products by the sum of squared centered inputs, then recover the intercept. Here the input mean is 0, the rating mean is 3, the product sum is 5.2, and the squared-input sum is 2.8. Therefore w = 5.2 / 2.8 = 13/7 and b = 3. This is a <strong>closed-form solution</strong>; the program does not need a gradient-descent loop to fit this line.</p><p>Other solvers can optimize the same objective. “Linear” describes how the learned coefficients enter the formula; transformed features can still represent curved relationships. Our unconstrained line does not guarantee outputs within 1–5 for arbitrary inputs or datasets. Clipping changes the delivered prediction and must be evaluated as part of the system. A fitted association also does not prove that changing someone's history would cause a rating to change. See scikit-learn's <a href="https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/linear_model.html">linear-model guide</a>.</p></details>

## 4. Measure regression errors in the right units

Define the prediction error as $e_i=\hat y_i-y_i$. If a model predicts 4 but the observed rating is 3, the error is +1. A signed average could let positive and negative errors cancel, so common losses use absolute or squared errors.

| Measure | Calculation | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MAE | Average of absolute errors | Typical absolute error in rating points |
| MSE | Average of squared errors | Rating points squared; larger errors matter more |
| RMSE | Square root of MSE | Error scale in rating points, sensitive to larger errors |

For predictions `[4, 2]` and observations `[3, 4]`, errors are `[1, −2]`. MAE is `(1 + 2) / 2 = 1.5`; MSE is `(1 + 4) / 2 = 2.5`; RMSE is about 1.5811. The units help you avoid treating MSE 2.5 as “off by 2.5 stars.” See [Google's regression-loss explanation](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/linear-regression/loss).

A **baseline** gives an improvement something to beat. Our regression baseline always predicts the training mean rating, 3. On the three validation events, its MSE is 2.6667; our unregularized line gets about 0.1878. That comparison describes these authored examples, not a real-world accuracy claim.

## 5. Fit a probability curve for a class

**Logistic regression**, despite its name, is commonly used for classification. It first computes a real-valued score, or **logit**, $z=wx+b$, then converts it through the **sigmoid** function:

$$
p=\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
$$

The output estimates $P(\text{thumbs-up}=1\mid x)$. A score of 0 maps to 0.5; a score of 1 maps to about 0.7311; negative scores map below 0.5. The sigmoid bounds the mathematical output between 0 and 1. It does not automatically establish accurate probabilities. See [Google's sigmoid walkthrough](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/logistic-regression/sigmoid-function).

Our fitted default classifier has $w\approx3.0351$ and $b\approx0$. For $s=0.75$, $x=0.5$, so $z\approx1.5175$ and $p\approx0.8202$. This probability concerns a thumbs-up; it is neither an 82% rating nor a guarantee about this viewer.

<SupervisedDiagrams kind="decision" />

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: calibration and more than two classes</summary><p>A probability estimate is <strong>calibrated</strong> when events assigned probabilities near 0.8 occur about 80% of the time over an appropriate set of cases. A sigmoid alone does not establish this; calibration must be checked on representative held-out data. Six synthetic training events are insufficient evidence. Distribution changes can also make previously useful probabilities unreliable.</p><p>For one-of-many classes, a model can produce one score per class and use <strong>softmax</strong> to turn them into probabilities summing to 1. For multilabel problems, separate sigmoid outputs can represent labels that may coexist; they need not sum to 1. The target meaning determines the appropriate output, loss, and decision policy. These models will connect to neural-network output layers later.</p></details>

## 6. Train classification with a probability-sensitive loss

**Binary cross-entropy**, also called binary log loss, compares an estimated probability $p$ with a label $y$ that is 0 or 1:

$$
\ell(y,p)=-[y\ln p+(1-y)\ln(1-p)].
$$

When $y=1$, the loss is $-\ln p$. Assigning the observed thumbs-up probability 0.8 costs about 0.2231; assigning it 0.2 costs about 1.6094. Confident mistakes receive a large penalty. With $y=0$, the relevant term is $-\ln(1-p)$. We average over training events.

**Accuracy** counts correct class decisions. It ignores whether a correct decision used probability 0.51 or 0.99. Log loss distinguishes those predictions, so two classifiers can have equal accuracy and different log losses. These are different questions, not interchangeable scores.

The workbook starts $w=b=0$ and takes 2,000 full-batch gradient steps with learning rate 0.3. Each step averages prediction-minus-label errors to update the parameters. This is an actual fit on the six training rows, not an authored probability table. The fixed step count is a teaching budget, not a general convergence guarantee. The loss connects to Chapter 8's cross-entropy and gradient descent; see [Google's logistic-loss guide](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/logistic-regression/loss-regularization).

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: the update you can inspect in Python</summary><p>For average binary cross-entropy, the derivative with respect to each logit is proportional to p − y. The workbook computes dw = mean((p − y) × x) + λw and db = mean(p − y), then updates w and b by subtracting 0.3 times their gradients. λ is the L2 strength explained below; the intercept is not penalized. Both gradients use the same old parameters before either update is applied.</p><p>The code evaluates log loss from logits with a stable equivalent expression, max(z, 0) − yz + log(1 + exp(−|z|)). This avoids computing log(0) after a probability rounds to 0 or 1. Finite-difference checks verify that the implemented gradient matches the objective. Numerical care preserves the calculation; it does not improve the data's representativeness.</p></details>

## 7. Choose a decision threshold separately

Our policy predicts thumbs-up when $p\ge t$, where $t$ is a threshold. At the default $t=0.5$, probability 0.8202 becomes thumbs-up. Raising the threshold to 0.9 changes it to thumbs-down. The weights and probability stay unchanged.

The **decision boundary** is where the class changes. At threshold 0.5, the logistic model's boundary is $wx+b=0$. With our nearly zero intercept, this is near $x=0$, or $s=0.5$. In multiple input dimensions, a basic logistic model has a linear boundary at a fixed threshold; other models can learn nonlinear boundaries.

A **false positive** predicts thumbs-up for an observed thumbs-down. A **false negative** misses an observed thumbs-up. Raising a threshold reduces the set predicted positive; it can avoid some unwanted recommendations while missing appealing ones. Choose that tradeoff using validation data and the product's costs, not the final test set. See scikit-learn's [threshold guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/classification_threshold.html).

## 8. Control flexibility and know the model families

**Underfitting** means the model misses relevant structure, even in training. **Overfitting** means it fits training-specific details that do not carry over well. A small training–validation gap alone does not prove a useful model; both sets can perform poorly.

**Regularization** discourages certain fitted solutions. Our L2 penalty is $\lambda w^2/2$. Larger $\lambda$ pushes the weight toward zero, making the response flatter. The regression objective is half the mean squared error plus this penalty; the classification objective is mean log loss plus the penalty. These scaling conventions matter when comparing implementations.

The weight and intercept are learned parameters. Strength $\lambda$, learning rate, and chosen model family are **hyperparameters**: settings chosen around training, commonly with validation. Excessive regularization can underfit; it never guarantees a better score.

<SupervisedDiagrams kind="tree" />

A **decision tree** learns feature-based questions and predictions for the resulting regions. In classification, splits can reduce label mixing, measured by quantities such as Gini impurity or entropy. Regression trees can reduce squared-error variation and predict leaf averages. Limiting depth or leaf size controls how finely the tree divides the data. See scikit-learn's [tree guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/tree.html).

<details className="optional-depth"><summary>Go deeper: a compact map of other supervised methods</summary><p><strong>k-nearest neighbors</strong> predicts from nearby stored examples: averaging their targets for regression or voting for classification. Its neighborhood size and distance definition matter. A <strong>support vector machine</strong> seeks a boundary with a large margin; kernels can represent nonlinear relationships. Scaling can materially affect both methods.</p><p><strong>Random forests</strong> combine randomized trees to reduce instability. <strong>Gradient boosting</strong> adds models sequentially to improve the ensemble's objective. <strong>Naive Bayes</strong> combines class and feature probabilities under a conditional-independence assumption. <strong>Neural networks</strong> learn composed transformations and are developed later in this handbook. These families offer different assumptions, flexibility, costs, and interpretability; none wins on every dataset. This chapter's executable models remain linear and logistic regression. See the primary <a href="https://scikit-learn.org/stable/supervised_learning.html">supervised-learning index</a> for the individual methods.</p></details>

## 12. Connect the pieces before choosing a bigger model

You can now trace **available inputs → fitted parameters → numeric prediction or probability → evaluation → application decision**. Keep each boundary explicit. A predicted high rating does not override Chapter 11's hard runtime constraint, and a thumbs-up probability does not establish streaming availability.

For a new task, define the target, establish a baseline, fit on training data, and compare suitable losses on held-out examples. Change model capacity and settings for a reason. More flexible algorithms cannot recover missing context merely by being more complex.
