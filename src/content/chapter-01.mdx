## 1. Begin with a problem, not a technology

Imagine an inbox receiving 150 emails a day. You want to catch spam while keeping legitimate messages easy to find. A phrase rule or a learned spam classifier might help. Choosing AI is a method decision; first define the problem.

| Question | Email-filter example |
|---|---|
| **Input:** What information is available? | Message text and available sender information |
| **Task:** What should the system do? | Identify likely spam |
| **Output:** What should it return? | A spam score and a flag-or-accept decision |
| **Success:** How will we judge it? | Catch spam while limiting false alarms on legitimate messages |

“Use AI” is vague. “Catch spam without hiding important messages” gives us two measurable goals. If a simple rule solves the problem reliably, a model may be unnecessary.

## 2. What does “artificial intelligence” mean?

**Artificial intelligence is the field of building computational systems that perform tasks involving perception, language, reasoning, planning, learning, or decision-making.** This is a working definition; the field's boundaries are debated. See [Poole and Mackworth's introduction](https://artint.info/3e/html/ArtInt3e.Ch1.S1.html) for an agent-oriented perspective.

AI includes search, logical rules, and learning methods. **Machine learning (ML)** is an approach within AI that fits models using data. The two terms are not interchangeable. [Google's ML introduction](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/intro-to-ml/what-is-ml) explains this learning approach.

**Capability** is a demonstrated ability on a task. **Reliability** is how consistently the system meets requirements across relevant situations. Recognizing some photographs does not prove an ability to plan trips—or even reliable recognition of every kind of photograph.

## 3. Look inside an AI application

A spam-classification model is one component. The complete application must receive messages, prepare their text, obtain scores, and decide which messages to flag.

<Diagram kind="anatomy" />

| Component | Role in the application |
|---|---|
| **Data and preparation** | Receive information and check or organize it before use |
| **Algorithm** | A specified computational procedure, such as a method for fitting a model |
| **Model** | A representation used to produce outputs; an ML model contains state fitted using data |
| **Application logic** | Apply policies, such as sending uncertain cases for review |
| **Interface** | Let people supply inputs and inspect results |
| **Evaluation** | Compare results with defined requirements |

The training algorithm is the **procedure**; the fitted model is the **resulting state used for predictions**. A model might output a spam-like score of 0.82. Moving a message to a spam folder is a separate action, controlled by application policy. A score alone does not grant permission to act.

## 4. The different kinds of work AI can do

These capabilities can be combined in one application:

| Capability | Meaning and example |
|---|---|
| **Perceive** | Extract information from sensory input, such as recognizing a handwritten digit |
| **Classify** | Assign a category, such as an email label, “spam” |
| **Estimate** | Produce a number, such as a predicted house price |
| **Rank** | Order candidates, such as search results ordered by relevance |
| **Generate** | Produce content, such as a summary of a study note |
| **Reason or solve** | Use relationships or constraints, such as checking whether two lessons fit a study session |
| **Plan** | Choose steps toward a goal, such as finding a robot’s route through a maze |
| **Learn** | Adjust a model from data or experience |

Three distinctions prevent confusion:

- **Prediction is broader than forecasting.** Identifying the language of an existing sentence predicts a label; forecasting estimates something in the future.
- **Receiving input is not perception.** Loading an image reads data; recognizing its objects interprets that data.
- **Planning is not execution.** Proposing a study schedule and adding events to a calendar are different operations.

## 5. Explicit rules versus learned patterns

A developer can write a spam rule directly:

```python
def should_flag(message):
    return "claim your prize" in message.lower()
```

This catches “CLAIM YOUR PRIZE now.” It misses different wording such as “A reward awaits; send your account details.” It also flags a legitimate warning that quotes “claim your prize.” The rule follows a phrase, not the message's intent.

In **supervised learning**, a training procedure uses examples paired with target answers, called **labels**. It adjusts model **parameters**—internal numerical values—to improve outputs against an objective. **Features** are input properties represented for the model, such as word counts or a numerical representation of the message.

<Diagram kind="rules" />

Rules provide directly specified behavior; learned models can capture patterns that are difficult to list manually. Both can fail and need testing. People still choose the data, model family, training procedure, and evaluation method.

**Do not equate rules with “never AI.”** Classical AI includes rule-based expert systems and search. A simple phrase filter is usually called automation; reasoning over a knowledge base may be called symbolic AI. Ask which parts are specified, which are learned, and how each is checked.

## 6. Training and inference: two different moments

**Training** fits model parameters using data. **Inference** runs the fitted model on an input. Here, “inference” means model execution, not necessarily logical deduction.

<Diagram kind="training" />

During training, a **loss** measures how poorly outputs satisfy the training objective. The learning procedure uses it to guide parameter updates. During inference, many deployed models keep their parameters fixed while producing outputs for new inputs.

A student who memorizes practice answers may fail unfamiliar questions. Likewise, **generalization** means useful performance on relevant new cases. Evaluate on examples that were not used to fit the model.

- **Overfitting:** fitting training data so closely that performance on new cases suffers.
- **Distribution shift:** incoming data differs from development data—for example, messages arrive in a previously untested language.

**New context and feedback are not automatically training.** Supplying a document changes the information available for a response. Correcting a category may merely record feedback. Whether either updates the model depends on the application.

## 7. Worked example: a score becomes a decision

A **threshold** is a cutoff that turns a score into a decision. Our rule is: **flag a message when its score is greater than or equal to the threshold**.

The eight messages below have synthetic labels and fixed illustrative scores. No model was trained for this demonstration. Higher scores mean “more spam-like,” but they are **not established probabilities**: 0.80 does not automatically mean an 80% chance of spam.

<ThresholdLab client:visible />

At the default threshold of **0.70**, A, B, and C are flagged:

| Result | Messages | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Correct spam detection | A, B | Spam is caught |
| **False positive** | C | A legitimate message is flagged |
| **False negative** | D, F | Spam is missed |
| Correct acceptance | E, G, H | Legitimate messages are left unflagged |

There are five correct decisions out of eight:

$$\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{correct decisions}}{\text{all decisions}} = \frac{5}{8} = 62.5\%$$

Dividing 5 by 8 gives 0.625; multiplying by 100 expresses it as a percentage. Accuracy counts correct decisions but does not distinguish the costs of different mistakes. [More on classification measures](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/classification/accuracy-precision-recall).

**Try 0.40:** all four spam messages are caught, but two legitimate messages are flagged. Six decisions are correct: 75% accuracy on this tiny dataset. At 0.90, only A is flagged, so more spam is missed.

**Only the policy changed; the scores did not.** Select a real application's threshold using representative validation examples and the costs of false alarms versus missed cases—not this teaching dataset.

## 8. Predictive tasks and generative tasks

**Predictive tasks** produce estimates, categories, scores, or rankings. **Generative tasks** produce content, such as text, images, audio, or code.

<Diagram kind="tasks" />

Consider a question submitted to a **personal learning assistant**: “What is overfitting?” It has this short fictional course note:

> **N01 · Overfitting:** A model can fit training examples too closely and perform poorly on new cases. This lesson has an estimated study time of 20 minutes. The notes do not list an exam date.

A predictive classifier might assign the question the topic **machine learning**. A generator might compose an explanation:

- **Supported draft:** “Overfitting means fitting training examples so closely that performance on new cases suffers [N01]. The note does not state your exam date.”
- **Unsupported draft:** “Your exam is Friday, and studying for 20 minutes guarantees mastery.” Neither the date nor guaranteed mastery follows from N01.

**Grounding** ties an answer to supporting information. A **hallucination**, here, is incorrect or unsupported generated content presented as an answer. Check support, important omissions, and preserved meaning—not just fluent writing.

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Optional connection: can generation also involve prediction?</summary>

Yes. A language model often generates text by repeatedly predicting a next **token**, a unit of text represented by the model. Its internal operation involves prediction; the application's output is generated content. The same model can also output one category label. Model mechanism and application task describe different levels.

</details>

## 9. Real-world walkthrough: a personal learning assistant

Our goal is a clear explanation from the learner’s notes, with sources and any missing information identified. Here is how the components work together:

<Diagram kind="assistant" />

1. **Specify the result.** Answer the learner’s question clearly; separate what the notes say from what remains unknown.
2. **Supply evidence.** Provide relevant notes, the question, and source references such as N01. **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** supplies retrieved information to support an answer; we will build it later.
3. **Generate a draft.** Request the required sections and explicit treatment of missing facts.
4. **Validate.** Code checks required fields, source identifiers, and calculations. These checks do not prove factual accuracy.
5. **Review claims.** A person checks the draft against evidence before approved use.
6. **Improve deliberately.** Use reviewed errors to revise prompts, data handling, or models; then evaluate on separate examples.

**A calculation can be correct while its interpretation is wrong.** A 30-minute study session minus N01’s estimated 20 minutes leaves 10 minutes. That establishes a time budget; it does not establish that the learner will master the topic in that time.

Students who revise more might also score higher on exams. **Correlation** is an association; **causation** is a claim that changing one factor affects another. That association alone does not prove that adding a particular revision session causes a higher score: prior knowledge and study methods may also matter.

## 10. When should you use AI?

A **baseline** is a simple reference solution used for comparison. Start there and add complexity when it measurably helps.

<Diagram kind="choice" />

| Need | Sensible first approach |
|---|---|
| Add known amounts; reject an empty field | Exact code, a formula, or a validation rule |
| Find a note with a known ID | Exact search |
| Find documents despite varied wording | Search; compare search by meaning (semantic retrieval) if needed |
| Categorize varied emails as spam or legitimate | Compare rules, a learned classifier, and a language model |
| Explain a topic from supplied study notes | A template or generative draft with evidence checks |
| Add events to a study calendar | Authorized application logic and an explicit approval policy |

Before choosing, ask: **Is the task clear? Is the evidence available? What does an error cost? Can we measure usefulness? Would a simpler method suffice?** Include verification effort: faster drafting is not a gain if checking the draft takes longer than doing the original task.

## 11. Limitations and misconceptions

| Misconception | Better understanding |
|---|---|
| Confident wording proves correctness | Tone is not evidence; verify claims |
| More data always helps | Irrelevant data, wrong labels, duplicates, or missing groups can reinforce errors |
| A learned model must vary its answer | Many are deterministic: the same input and model state can give the same output; sampling can introduce variation |
| One successful example proves readiness | Test ordinary, difficult, incomplete, and unanswerable cases |
| A good average score is enough | Inspect the error types and whether the test resembles real use |

Use public or synthetic data for initial practice. For workplace applications, use appropriate data permissions and an approved environment. Models still need the surrounding validation, access controls, and review introduced above.

## 12. Optional Python lab: inspect a simple baseline

The phrase rule from Section 5 can be checked against four examples. It gets two right, misses differently worded spam, and flags a legitimate warning. **The labels check the rule; they do not train it.**

<details className="optional-depth">
<summary>Optional lab: code, output, and a small experiment</summary>

Run this with Python 3; no additional packages or account are needed.

```python
def should_flag(message):
    return "claim your prize" in message.lower()

examples = [
    ("CLAIM YOUR PRIZE now", True),
    ("A reward awaits; send your account details", True),
    ("Training: avoid messages saying 'claim your prize'", False),
    ("Tomorrow's project meeting agenda", False),
]

correct = 0
for message, expected in examples:
    predicted = should_flag(message)
    correct += int(predicted == expected)
    print(f"predicted={predicted}, expected={expected}")

print(f"Correct decisions: {correct}/{len(examples)}")
```

Expected output:

```text
predicted=True, expected=True
predicted=False, expected=True
predicted=True, expected=False
predicted=False, expected=False
Correct decisions: 2/4
```

`int(True)` is 1 and `int(False)` is 0, so the counter increases for correct results. `len(examples)` counts the examples.

Add another trigger phrase, predict what changes, then test new messages—including a legitimate message quoting that phrase. A broader rule can catch more spam and create more false alarms. These four cases demonstrate behavior; they do not estimate real-world performance.

</details>

## 15. Revision notes and glossary

- **AI is broader than ML:** it also includes search, rules, and planning.
- **Define the task first:** input, task, output, and success criteria.
- **Training fits; inference uses.** New context, feedback, and threshold changes are separate mechanisms.
- **A model is one component.** Policies turn its outputs into actions.
- **Evidence matters:** check generated claims and test on relevant new cases.
- **Compare with a baseline:** inspect different errors and their practical costs.
